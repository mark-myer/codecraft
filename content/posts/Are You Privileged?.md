# Are You Privileged?

![[30650ac1412c9b8aba49a1d9f24aafa7_MD5.jpg]]
Eugene stepped on the throttle as his VW zoomed down the interstate. He listened to the throaty rumble and remarked to his sister Amy sitting beside him: “Feels great to be able to get out and drive again after staying mostly at one place for three weeks.”

She nodded, looking out into the dark stretch of road ahead. “For sure, although we did get out for cottage meeting the second Sunday.”

“You’re right.” Eugene clicked on the auto-highbeam switch. “Bible school’s great, but right now I’m enjoying my own set of wheels again.” He grinned at Amy and glanced at the instrument panel. “Wow, it looks like we need fuel soon. Thirty miles to E.”

“There’s a Pilot travel center next exit.” Amy motioned toward the glowing sign.

“Five dollars and ninety-nine cents per gallon!” Eugene flicked the turn signal. “That’s insane!”

“You could be paying $4.19 if you had bought a Honda,” Amy teased.

“A diesel car is still worth it,” Eugene countered. “But my, that’s way higher than when we went to Bible School. People kept saying fuel prices jumped because of the war, but I didn’t think it could be this bad.” He opened the driver’s door and pulled out his Platinum Rewards credit card.

Several minutes later, Eugene pulled away from the pump. “Ninety dollars! Absolutely crazy!” He shook his head as he crammed the receipt into his wallet. I’m not sure what the US thinks it’s doing, getting involved in another war.”

“Just be glad there aren’t drones overhead and bombs raining down.” Amy pulled out a bag of Skittles as the car smoothly accelerated onto the interstate.

“You’re right.” Eugene merged onto the highway. “But this fuel price is about as bad.”

Meanwhile, in Tehran, Iran:

Dariush gripped the handlebars of his aging motorcycle and eased into the fuel line. The line stretched half a block -- it always did, now that the war had gutted supplies. He turned off the engine and waited, pushing forward a few feet at a time.

Nobody talked much at the fuel line anymore.

When he finally reached the pump, the agent glanced at his ID card. Rationed amount only. And the price had jumped again -- to sixty-six percent higher than six months ago. That was before the Strait closed and made everything worse. Dariush had stopped doing the math. It only made him angry.

He rode home through the rutted streets sooty with smog, past the bakery where a cluster of women stood with cloth bags, waiting to buy bread. Bread had more than doubled in price since last year, along with eggs and rice. His wife Nasrin had stopped buying beef entirely months ago and now chicken was nearly beyond reach too.

Inside the apartment, she looked up from the stove. "Power goes out a ten," she said, the way another woman might say *dinner is ready*. She'd memorized the weekly blackout schedule, and kept buckets at the sink for the hours when the water pressure dropped without warning.

"I know." Dariush sat down heavily at the table.

His ride-hailing shifts barely covered fuel costs anymore. The numbers had stopped making sense in February. On the table sat a partial prescription: two of the four medicines the doctor had ordered. They'd bought what they could afford and left the rest. The pharmacist had shrugged. Half his shelves were bare anyway.

As he waited for supper, Dariush stared at the wall. A jagged crack ran half-way up, a souvenir from an airstrike last winter. He thought of how it symbolized their lives. Nothing made sense anymore. They had survived that strike, though. His cousin in the south had not.

Nasrin set a bowl of plain rice in front of him. "There's no cooking oil."

"It's okay."

Karen pulled her sweater tighter and smoothed her dress as she waited for the service to begin. *Why do they always keep it so cold in here?* she mouthed to Beth. Beth shrugged and turned her attention to the front as the song leader announced the opening number.

"Come let us all unite to sing--God is love." The song, carried by a hundred voices, rolled through the sanctuary. It filled the cathedral ceiling and drifted out the open windows (which also let in the chilly air). Karen smiled. Oh, the joys of singing together.  Karen forgot the 69* temperature.

Fifteen minutes later, Karen sat in the spacious basement Sunday school classroom, opening her quarterly as class began. The studies in John were interesting. Today's lesson was chapter 15. Karen followed along in her quarterly as the class read the lesson scope. 

*They will persecute you.*

She clicked her pen and underlined the words neatly, like she usually did when something seemed important. The class discussion moved on to verse 13, *Greater love hath no man than this.* Someone shared an anecdote from a comment a neighbor had made about their church community. The class moved on.

From somewhere deeper in the basement, the aroma of scalloped potatoes mixed with the delightful fragrance of herb bread. This was fifth Sunday--time for fellowship meal.

After the service, she met Beth by the coat rack.

"Good Sunday school discussion," Beth said as they joined the line headed toward the basement.

"Definitely." Karen dropped her Bible on the pile on the hall shelf. "I still they think they should do something about the thermostat, though. Did you notice it never warmed up?"

Beth laughed. "Talk to Mervin. He's the janitor this month."

On the other side of the world, in an Asian city Karen had never heard of, other people met in Jesus' name. 

There was no hearty singing, no Sunday School quarterlies, and no fragrant fellowship meal.

They read the same verse that morning: *They will persecute you.*

None of them underlined it and moved on.

Li Jing checked her phone furtively as she quietly climbed the concrete steps to the fifth-floor apartment. *Good, no warning messages.* Her mind flashed back to last Sunday, when church had been cancelled due to a rumor that the authorities had discovered the meeting place. So far, it seemed to be just that: a rumor. Still, one could never tell.

The hallway was dim. She approached door number 253 and knocked twice, waited a moment and knocked twice--the signal. The door quickly opened. Li stepped in as Sister Mei Ling shut the door, making sure no one was in the hall. 

She looked around the small living room. Mismatched chairs and a folding bench crowded the small space, but all twelve of the small group had managed to attend this service. 

Someone pressed a hand-written song sheet into her hand. There were no hymnals, nothing printed in quantity. Brother Shen had the only Bible their group owned, holding it on his lap like something someone might take from him, because it was.

They sang together, not in whispers, but quietly, with the volume of people who are aware that walls have ears. Sitting beside Li, Sister Shen closed her eyes and sang full volume like she always did. Li had always loved that about her. 

After the singing, Brother Shen opened the Bible. "Has anyone been contacted this week? Anything unusual?"

A young man raised his hand slightly. "My employer questioned me last Thursday. He wondered where I spend Sundays." He said it matter-of-factly, without drama, but everyone knew what it meant.

Brother Shen nodded. "We will pray for you this morning, that you will be faithful. We pray for Zhang Chen and Shu-Hau as well." Several heads bowed at the names. "We don't know where they were taken, but our prayers follow them." He began to read his text for the Bible study: John 15. 

*They will persecute you.* He read it like a man familiar with the phrase. He didn't explain it or gloss it over. It described their lives.

Tantalizing aromas filled the kitchen as Tyler pulled back his chair and sat down. He closed his eyes for a brief moment and took a deep breath, filling his lungs with ham and potato stew scent mixed with fresh homemade bread.

 Kendrick tapped his arm. "Hey, Tyler, did Mr. Anderson come to the shop today and pick up his backhoe?" 
 
 Tyler grinned and tweaked Kendrick's ear. "Nope, I've got to fix the last broken steering joint yet. Want to ride with me tomorrow afternoon when you come after school?"

"Yes!"

"Let's pray," Daddy directed. Tyler could hear the clock tick for a minute as each of the eight heads around the table bowed to give thanks. After prayer, the noise rose to usual supper-time level. 

Randal passed the baked beans to Tyler. "We're learning about fighting fires in science class. And Sister Julia brought old fire extinguishers for us to practice with."

"Nice." Tyler reached for the salt. "Was it fun?"

"Yeah. We started a fire in the parking lot and all took a turn spraying it out. The last people to have a turn were me and Micah and Kayla."

Kendrick bounced in his chair "And they sprayed and sprayed the fire till the fire extinguishers were all used up!"

Randall frowned at his brother. "I wanted to tell that!" His face turned around into a grin as he remembered. "Till we were done, all the pieces of wood had a layer of white dust this thick!" 

Tyler joined in as the laughter bounced around the table. This was home. This was his family.

Across the evergreen hedge from Tyler's home, thirteen-year-old Jackson was eating alone. Mom had left at six o'clock for her evening shift. She had left a box of macaroni and cheese on the counter with a sticky note: *Heat this up. Love you.* Jackson had been heating up mac and cheese ever since he was nine, so that part was fine.

He set the bowl in the microwave, adding a little extra butter like she had shown him. Let's see: Mom would be working at Walmart this evening, since it was Thursday. She worked there Tuesday, Thursday, and Saturday, night shift. Monday, Wednesday, and Fridays found her at the dry cleaner in town. Weekends, she was home, but tired. Usually the kind of tired that stays in bed till 10:00 in the morning and doesn't talk much.

He walked over to the couch with his steaming bowl. Why sit at the table anyway when it was just him at home?

At the other end of the den, the TV screen lit up as the evening talk show came on. As he ate the mac and cheese, Tyler watched the show, except he didn't watch it. His thoughts wandered to his Dad.

Dad lived 4 hours away in Cypress Lake City, in his own apartment since he and Mom were legally separated now. Tyler saw him on weekends, except when something came up, which it often did. It didn't really matter though. Dad had never had much time for his son. Not with him managing one of the fastest-growing startups in Silicon Valley. 

Jackson wasn't unhappy. Not exactly. His Dad would buy anything for him. But something inside him ached in a way he couldn't explain.

Jackson set his empty bowl on the coffee table and pulled a blanket over his legs. His phone dinged with a game notification. He tapped it.

At 9:30, Jackson still sat on the couch, headphones on, absorbed in zapping space aliens. The house was quiet. Not a peaceful kind of quiet, just empty. Mom texted: *Home by 10. There's ice-cream in the freezer.* Tyler sent the thumbs-up emoji and didn't get the ice-cream.
